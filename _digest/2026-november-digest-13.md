Balčus, Zane, Renata Šukaitytė. "Baltic Narratives About the Soviet Past: The Documentary Cinema of Ilze Burkovska-Jacobsen and Giedrė Žickytė." *Central European Narratives:  On 20th and 21st Century Non-Fiction in the Region*, Palgrave Macmillan Cham. [<span style="background-color: #5a96d0; color: white; padding: 0.25em 0.4em; border-radius: 0.25rem; font-size: 75%; line-height: 1;">Open Access</span>](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-032-32529-7_10)  
<details markdown="1"><summary>Annotation</summary>
This chapter examines the creative non-fiction films of two prominent Baltic documentary filmmakers, Lithuanian director Giedrė Žickytė and Latvian director Ilze Burkovska-Jacobsen. Both employ distinctive visual and narrative strategies to explore life under the communist regime and the Soviet legacy in Baltic societies. The chapter focuses on My Favourite War (2020) and My Mother’s Farm (2008) by Burkovska-Jacobsen, and The Jump (2020), Master and Tatyana (2015), and How We Played the Revolution (2012) by Žickytė. These films employ personal and affective approaches to historical representation and can be understood as microhistorical narratives, as autobiographical perspectives are placed at the centre of their historical inquiry. By combining personal experience, archival materials, and creative narrative strategies, the films help renew collective memory of the recent past and make historical experiences accessible to audiences who did not directly live through these events. Drawing on theoretical approaches to creative non-fiction proposed by Philip Gerard and Sheila Curran Bernard, the chapter examines how the intersection of subjective, analytical, and critical approaches contributes to an aesthetic experience of everyday life during the Soviet period. It also considers how these cinematic strategies differ from traditional reportage-style or expository documentaries, as well as from other forms of creative non-fiction.
</details>  

Skarzauskiene, Aelita, Monika Mačiulienė, Kristina Kovaitė. "Beyond digitisation: latent demand and the public reach of digital cultural heritage in Lithuania." *International Journal of Heritage Studies*, 14 Nov. 2026 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/13527258.2026.2764704).  
<details markdown="1"><summary>Abstract</summary>
This article asks whether supply-side investment in digital cultural heritage infrastructure produces equitable population engagement. Drawing on a nationally representative survey of Lithuanian adults (n = 1,004, September–October 2025), we identify four distinct engagement patterns: engaged-and-valued (40.7%), latent demand (22%), disengaged-indifferent (22.2%), and engaged-but- indifferent (15.1%). The latent demand stratum comprises 22% of the population who value digital heritage but do not engage with it. Contrary to assumptions about digital inequality, latent demand is not concentrated among the economically or educationally disadvantaged. Logistic regression analysis shows that latent demand membership is significantly associated with higher digital skills (OR 1.63, p < 0.001) and better subjective financial status (OR 1.70, p = 0.004), but not with age, education, or settlement type. Latent demand respondents also agree more strongly that easier-to-find content and museum- or library-run platforms would increase their use. Disengagement, by contrast, is concentrated among the least educated, older, rural and poorer residents. The value-engagement gap extends across civic and environmental participation domains, indicating a structural pattern in digital participation. These findings reframe heritage policy evaluation. Infrastructure provision and population reach operate as distinct dimensions. Effective heritage sustainability requires measuring both supply and citizen- side capability through periodic population surveys. Current evaluation frameworks measure supply; they do not measure whether populations can or will engage with that supply.
</details>  

Ventsel, Andreas. "Re‐Signifying Soviet Monuments: State Self‐Description, Memory Politics and Ontological Security in Estonia." *Nations and Nationalism*, 5 Nov. 2026 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/nana.70118).  
<details markdown="1"><summary>Abstract</summary>
Since the restoration of independence, Soviet-era monuments in Estonia have remained contested sites through which historical legitimacy, political belonging and national identity are publicly negotiated. Following Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, these monuments acquired renewed salience as Estonia moved to regulate symbols that had become flashpoints in struggles over memory and the symbolic order of the nation-state. This article examines how Soviet monuments in Estonia are re-signified. Drawing on a data-driven typology-building approach and an analytical reconstruction of salient interventions since 1991, it develops a fourfold typology of re-signification strategies and identifies the conditions under which they de-escalate contestation, intensify it or displace it onto other symbolic sites and issues. The article shows how semiotic mechanisms of state self-description shape these processes and argues that monument re-signification is central to the symbolic reproduction of political community under conditions of ontological insecurity.
</details>  

Kačerauskas, Tomas. "The political as crisis and war: the case of Lithuania." *Studies in East European Thought*, 15 Oct. 2026 [<span style="background-color: #5a96d0; color: white; padding: 0.25em 0.4em; border-radius: 0.25rem; font-size: 75%; line-height: 1;">Open Access</span>](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11212-026-09927-x).  
<details markdown="1"><summary>Abstract</summary>
Using Lithuania as a case, the paper develops Carl Schmitt’s idea that the political is inseparable from war and crisis. At the same time, the question arises as to whether democracy is conducive to inciting war both within and outside the state. The following theses are developed. 1. Politics “produces” enemies, which are necessary for permanent war, which ensures the political. 2. Enemies are demonized and hunted, but without them, the political would disappear, an aspect of which is the desired “concentration” of society under the flag of the ruling party. 3. The society of the spectacle perfectly serves to implement the principles of all-encompassing monitoring, self-censorship, and “production” of enemies. 4. In a democratic environment, responsibility is erased, with decisions being made by committees, commissions, and the majority of the parliament, advised by “specialists” who are educated in a certain direction to achieve economic or technological progress. 5. The digital panopticon enables control and surveillance of people who betray themselves as enemies of society on social networks. A paradoxical situation arises when the political is supported by “producing” enemies, but their extermination threatens to bury democracy. It is argued that it is not so much democracy itself as the symbiosis of democracy, power, and globalization that is responsible for this situation. A comparative critical analysis of ideas is applied, combined with a case study.
</details>  

Danylevych, Kateryna. "The representation of the invasion of Estonia in 1918 and 1940 in contemporary Russian school history textbooks." *Journal of Baltic Studies*, 15 Oct. 2026 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/01629778.2026.2749819).  
<details markdown="1"><summary>Abstract</summary>
This article analyzes how the events of the Estonian War of Independence in 1918–1920 and the annexation of Estonia in 1940 are presented in Russian secondary school history textbooks published between 1997 and 2023. It explores the evolution of interpretative frameworks shaping these narratives and assesses the presence of pluralistic approaches to the past within Russia’s general secondary education system, which in recent years has increasingly shifted toward a monistic model. Particular attention is paid to how new narratives reflect the rhetoric of the Russian political elite, as well as the key argumentative strategies used in attempts to legitimize or justify Russia’s actions.
</details>  

Jonkutė, Viktorija. "The poetic imagery of the post-Soviet transition in the Lithuanian and Latvian cultural press, 1988–1992." *Studies in East European Thought*, vol. 78, no. 5, Oct. 2026 [<span style="background-color: #5a96d0; color: white; padding: 0.25em 0.4em; border-radius: 0.25rem; font-size: 75%; line-height: 1;">Open Access</span>](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11212-025-09763-5).  
<details markdown="1"><summary>Abstract</summary>
The present article focuses on images of the post-Soviet transition represented in the Lithuanian and Latvian cultural press. It proved to be a multifaceted process of change and an intense, dynamic, and comprehensive transformation. The collapse of the Soviet Union was one of the main factors in the development of all post-Soviet states. In addition, the national movement (known as “the Singing Revolution” or Awakening) resulted in the restoration of independence in Lithuania and Latvia after almost fifty years of Soviet occupation. It also manifested itself as a reconstructive process, as one of the main tasks was to recover authentic memory, and to restore the heritage of the first Republic and the interwar period. The Lithuanian and Latvian cultural press of the period from 1988 to 1992 employed various phrases to describe ongoing events. A certain type of poetic representation and symbolic imagery of the transition appeared in contemporary reflections. Most of them were metaphors from the natural environment (flowing river, sprouting grass, stone, growing child, injured animals, limited space, etc.). The aim of this article is to introduce and compare some of the major semantic and poetic conceptualizations conveyed through these cultural and political images.  

</details>  

Bógdał-Brzezińska,, Agnieszka, Jan A. Wendt. *Small States in the Shatter Belt: Demographic and Geopolitical Challenges of the Baltic States*. Routledge, 2025. [link](https://www.routledge.com/Small-States-in-the-Shatter-Belt-Demographic-and-Geopolitical-Challenges-of-the-Baltic-States/Bogdal-Brzezinska-Wendt/p/book/9781041194040)  
<details markdown="1"><summary>Annotation</summary>
The book aims to highlight the challenges faced by small states located in zones of geopolitical instability. Drawing on Saul P. Cohen's theoretical concept of the shatter belt, it discusses a geopolitical region characterized by significant instability, owing especially to the influence of neighbouring powers.  
  
The book defines the challenges related to the geopolitical position of Baltic Europe and describes them as manifestations of the shatter belt. It details the historical conditions of the small Baltic states: Lithuania, Latvia, and Estonia, with reference to their geopolitical position within the Russian sphere of influence and after the collapse of the USSR. This study also focuses on the exploitation of national minorities as one the main challenges to the national security of the Baltic states.  
  
As a pioneering study on the role of small states as peacekeepers, allies, and members of international organizations, this book will be a valuable resource for students and doctoral candidates in international relations, geopolitics, and political geography. It will also be useful to experts, journalists, and policymakers.
</details>  

Gluzman, Simon. "Academic Evgeny Tarle and the Geopolitics of Soviet Borders: From Low-Parameter Imperial Engineering to Post-Tarle Metastability." *preprint*, November 2026 [<span style="background-color: #5a96d0; color: white; padding: 0.25em 0.4em; border-radius: 0.25rem; font-size: 75%; line-height: 1;">Open Access</span>](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Simon-Gluzman/publication/417933214_Academic_Evgeny_Tarle_and_the_Geopolitics_of_Soviet_Borders_From_Imperial_Engineering_to_Post-Tarle_Metastability/links/6afa44fcdc41aa6ac311b2ac/Academic-Evgeny-Tarle-and-the-Geopolitics-of-Soviet-Borders-From-Imperial-Engineering-to-Post-Tarle-Metastability.pdf).  
<details markdown="1"><summary>Abstract</summary>
The post-World War II territorial architecture of Eurasia was neither an accidental byproduct of battlefield lines nor a purely ideological imposition. Rather, it reflected a classical “Sovereign–Sage” optimization executed by Joseph Stalin and his preeminent diplomatic historian, Academic Evgeny Tarle. Synthesizing structural insights from Central European and Black Sea security models, this preprint examines how Tarle translated 19th-century European balance-of-power Realpolitik and Tsarist imperial restitution into the spatial geometry of 1945. We trace Tarle’s paradoxical 1941 rehabilitation of Great Russian patriotic nationalism, his blueprint for the Oder–Neisse border translocation, his historical framing of the Turkish Straits and Far Eastern perimeters, and his putative Realpolitik warning against granting the Ukrainian SSR a separate founding seat in the United Nations—an institutional concession that functioned as a delayed geopolitical mine detonating in 1991. Finally, we formalize the game-theoretic boundary conditions of the Sovereign–Sage dyad, explaining how the post-1991 emergence of sovereign middle powers transformed static buffers into two coupled metastable rectangles linked through Ukraine, and why Stalin’s post-1948 opala against Tarle and his cosmopolitan intellectual cohort ultimately stripped the Soviet system of its analytical self-correction.
</details>  

Pirrie, Della. *We had our own abroad here, one of a kind: Interactions with Music in Estonia (1975-2000)*. November 2026. University of Helsinki. [<span style="background-color: #5a96d0; color: white; padding: 0.25em 0.4em; border-radius: 0.25rem; font-size: 75%; line-height: 1;">Open Access</span>](http://urn.fi/URN:NBN:fi:hulib-202611105184)  
<details markdown="1"><summary>Annotation</summary>
This is an interview based study that examines how individuals interacted with music in Estonia between 1975 and 2000, from the end of the Soviet Period and the years following the regaining of independence. Estonia’s history with music is a site of great interest for scholars of social movements, cultural studies, and national identity. Furthermore, the division between Estonians and Russian-speakers is an oft-used case study for examining ethnic minorities, national identity, and integration. The idea of ‘The West’ has also seen academic attention in analyses of late-Soviet society. Given the relevance of music to Estonia as a country, the existence of differing historical perspectives in Estonia, and Estonia’s unique association with the West in the USSR, I sought to combine these three topics by conducting semi-structured interviews and a key informant interview. Six interviews and a key informant interview were conducted, with a mix of Estonians and Russian-speaking Estonians, to understand what music meant in their lives and how they accessed music and information more broadly.
</details>

It was a machine invented by Edison that was already outdated by the time it was created. The true future of cinema belonged to the big screen, which offered audiences a far more powerful experience.  
Estonia's first building constructed specifically and exclusively for showing films opened for the 1907 summer season in Pärnu, opposite the Bath House. Traveling cinema operator Karl Stephan, arriving from Riga, began showing "moving pictures" here, and the cinema was named "Electro-Biograph."  
The exhibition presents the story of Pärnu's cinemas through words as well as moving and still images—from these early pioneers and built (or never built) cinema buildings up to the present day.

## Call for Papers

**Baltic Screen Media Review** - Onscreen Intimacy and Vulnerability  
Deadline: 2027-03-05  
Intimacy and vulnerability on screen are subject to ever-changing norms and expectations, and, chiastically, the same expectations and norms are subject to ever changing onscreen vulnerability and intimacy. Things are changing and there are many examples: the increasingly wide-spread employment of intimacy co-ordinators and the concomitant changes in professional standards; the way that intimacy is mediated through social media platforms which reshape human connections by turning apps and algorithms into channels for often intimate and vulnerable personal expression; the proliferation of intimacy, nudity, and sexuality on prestige television programmes; and the backlash or counter movements these changes bring about. These and many other developments raise important questions and curiosities in the current decade’s film, media, and performance landscapes: how are intimacy and vulnerability discussed, shaped, and reconstituted in modern culture? Where does one's responsibility lie when encountering/working with screen-based or performance-based intimacy and vulnerability? How might we better understand the effects of mediation on intimacy and vulnerability?  
  
For this special issue, we take a broader view of intimacy derived from the Latin, intimus, (meaning ‘innermost’) and invite contributions which consider intimacy as more than sexual but as something emotional, physical, intellectual, and social. Intimacy might be what is found at the heart of sexual encounters between lovers but it can just as easily be found in a quiet conversation with a friend. Meanwhile, vulnerability is a quality which is increasingly valorised in the modern world and allows for intimacy in all of the aforementioned spheres but is also both a voluntary and often involuntary quality which invites consideration of how cultural production navigates either eventuality responsibly.
