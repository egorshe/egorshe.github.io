## Publications 📚

Davoliūtė, Violeta,  Dovilė Budrytė. *The Routledge Handbook of Memory in Post-Socialist Europe*. Routledge, 2027. [link](https://www.routledge.com/The-Routledge-Handbook-of-Memory-in-Post-Socialist-Europe/Budryte-Davoliute/p/book/9781032352541)  
<details markdown="1"><summary>Annotation</summary>
This handbook shows how memory operates simultaneously as lived experience, institutional practice, geopolitical weapon, and resource for imagining the future. It explores decolonial and postcolonial approaches, memory laws, oral history, nostalgia, testimony and digital memory-making.  
  
Spanning the aftermath of the Second World War, the socialist and post-socialist periods, and Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine, the volume covers post-socialist Europe and relevant diasporic and transnational contexts. Its case studies include the Holocaust commemoration in Belarus, museums in the former Yugoslavia, the Holodomor, Crimean Tatar cultural diplomacy, Poland’s Museum of the Second World War, Russia’s Memorial society, and Ukrainian virtual war museums. The thirty-two chapters gathered here collectively demonstrate that memory in the region is not a secondary reflection of politics but a constitutive dimension of political life itself.  
  
The book will be of interest for advanced undergraduate students, graduate students and scholars working on topics related to memory in the region, while remaining accessible to non-specialist readers.
</details>  

Starkutė, Ugnė Barbora, Anna Ewa Pilarczyk-Palaitis. "“Authentically” ethnic? Coloniality as expectations of “authenticity” towards ethnic minorities." *Journal of Baltic Studies*, 14 Sept. 2026 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/01629778.2026.2732984).  
<details markdown="1"><summary>Abstract</summary>
In this article, we explore how the epistemic character of coloniality is manifested in identity politics toward ethnic minorities through the expectations of ‘authenticity.’ Two different cases are examined: the indigenous Sámi in Finland and the Polish ‘accidental diaspora’ in Lithuania. These cases are based on ethnographic fieldwork conducted from 2016 to 2024 in Finnish Sápmi, and in Lithuania and Poland. The article aims to show that oppressive ‘authenticity’ affects both indigenous and national minorities. It also contributes to discussions on the relationship between nation-state, ethnic minority rights, and the sustainable coexistence of diverse ethnic and national groups.
</details>  

Hiršs, Andris, Andrejs Balodis, Ainārs Kamoliņš. "De-Sovietization as metanoia: rewriting the history of philosophy in Latvia." *Studies in East European Thought*, 8 Sept. 2026 [<span style="background-color: #5a96d0; color: white; padding: 0.25em 0.4em; border-radius: 0.25rem; font-size: 75%; line-height: 1;">Open Access</span>](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11212-026-09895-2).  
<details markdown="1"><summary>Abstract</summary>
The study interprets the de-Sovietization of Latvian philosophy as a complex process of intellectual reorientation rather than a radical rupture. It examines public debates on the role of philosophers, philosophy textbooks, the transformation of philosophical terminology, and the expansion of translations of Western philosophical works. The article also analyzes the rewriting of the historiography of philosophy in Latvia. Together, these developments suggest that the philosophical landscape after 1991 was shaped by processes of reinterpretation, selective continuity, and institutional adaptation, demonstrating how elements of Soviet philosophical practice were simultaneously rejected, transformed, and partially preserved within the emerging intellectual culture.
</details>  

Jurgutienė, Aušra, Eva Eglāja-Kristsone. *Baltic Literature and Russian Imperialism *. Brill, 2026. [link](https://brill.com/display/title/72409)  
<details markdown="1"><summary>Annotation</summary>
With Russia’s 2022 invasion of Ukraine reviving imperial rhetoric, Baltic Literature and Russian Imperialism explores how literature has long resisted domination. Twenty scholars uncover how Latvian, Lithuanian, and Estonian writers responded to Tsarist, Soviet, and post-Soviet forms of Russian power. From censored novels and forced Russification to suppressed memory and cultural survival, this book traces how literature exposes injustice, preserves national identity, and challenges the myths of liberation. Drawing on rare archival sources and regional postcolonial approaches, this timely volume reveals why the cultural fight for freedom, waged with words, is as urgent now as ever.
</details>  

Brüggemann, Karsten. "The Baltic “Our Abroad” in Russian Imperial Imagination: Continuity and Change from the Late Russian Empire to the Early Soviet Union." *Spatial Dimensions of Russian and Soviet Imperial Ideologies and Practices (1689–2024)*, Brill. [link](https://brill.com/display/book/9783657705368/BP000020.xml)  
<details markdown="1"><summary>Annotation</summary>
Studies focusing on the Baltic Socialist Soviet Republics often mention that this region was perceived by many contemporaries as a “Soviet West” or as “our abroad”. What is often overlooked is that this was no Soviet invention. Already during tsarist times, the Baltic provinces were seen as more “Western” and more “European”, essentially a “Russian Germany”. This chapter explores the origins of this image and discusses how Russian nationalism established an alternative reading of the Baltic littoral “otherness”. Instead of being a marker of the empire’s true Europeananness, they were transformed into a potentially disloyal “German bastion” against Russia. This chapter examines further the changes in the perception of the region during the interwar period, when it became a reality abroad. Early Soviet texts about the Baltic states replaced ethnic stereotypes with class stereotypes, portraying them as a bridgehead of imperialism and “fascist” states suppressing their proletariat. As I argue in this chapter, the early Soviet view thus essentially continued with the patterns established by Russian nationalism in the nineteenth century. Only after 1939 and the establishment of Red Army bases on the territory of the Baltic states, they were made into friendly neighbours, before the annexation of 1940 once again made them a potential “our West”.
</details>
