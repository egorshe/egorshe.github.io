Gaižutytė-Filipavičienė, Žilvinė. "Representing Nature in Soviet Lithuanian Documentary Cinema: Ideology and Ecological Consciousness." *Studies in East European Thought*, 19 Oct. 2026 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11212-026-09929-9).  
<details markdown="1"><summary>Abstract</summary>
This article examines Soviet Lithuanian nature documentary from the late 1950s to the late 1980s within the context of socialist modernization and contemporary Anthropocene discourse. While Western nature documentary has been widely studied, films produced in the Soviet bloc remain underexamined. Drawing on an archival corpus of Lithuanian documentaries and newsreels, the study analyzes how nature was represented across shifting ideological and aesthetic frameworks. Combining documentary mode theory, narrative analysis, and discourse-based approaches, the article asks whether these films functioned primarily as instruments of modernization and ideological power or whether they also retained a degree of aesthetic autonomy. It demonstrates that Lithuanian nature documentary operated within a dual logic: expository and reportage films framed nature as a resource to be rationally managed, whereas poetic, observational, and later ecoactivist works emphasized ecological responsibility and contributed to the politicization of environmental discourse in the 1980s. The study argues that Soviet Lithuanian nature documentary offers a significant archive for understanding the historical formation of environmental consciousness in late socialism.
</details>  

Liutikas, Darius. "Cemetery Tourism in Lithuania: Landscapes, Cemetery Development and Visitor Motives." *Cemetery Tourism*, 12 Oct. 2026 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1079/9781836993797.0008).  
<details markdown="1"><summary>Abstract</summary>
This chapter analyses the details of cemetery landscapes in Lithuania and associated visitation practices. Its aim is to discuss and examine the principal elements of cemeteries as landscape features, including their spatial distribution (number and occupied area), landscape characteristics, funerary monument architecture and the motives for visiting cemeteries. Various classifications are applied: all burial places in Lithuania are divided into five categories; motives for visiting cemeteries are grouped into nine categories; and types of monuments are systematically classified. Particular attention is devoted to motives for visiting cemeteries and to cemetery tourism. The practice of visiting cemeteries is analysed through a sociological lens. The cemetery is considered a socio-cultural space, where visitation depends on a variety of personal, collective, ideological and spiritual factors. The author argues that it is important to preserve the notion of cemeteries as sacred places or highly esteemed sites.
</details>  

Navickaitė, Rasa. "‘like a Nest of Homosexuals:’ Tracing Homophobia in Post-socialist Lithuanian Print Media (1994–2004)." *Journal of Baltic Studies*, 9 Oct. 2026 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/01629778.2026.2740537).  
<details markdown="1"><summary>Abstract</summary>
This article examines the historical roots and discursive developments of homophobia in Lithuanian print media from 1994 to 2004, a decade following the decriminalization of homosexuality, and leading up to Lithuania’s accession to the European Union. The article demonstrates how media, largely following Soviet-era stereotypes, linked male homosexuality with disease, secrecy, pedophilia, and Western moral degradation. It shows that since 2000 there was a rise in targeted homophobic campaigns, especially by the Respublika publications group. Furthermore, the Catholic Church’s role as a moral authority opposed to LGBTI rights gradually grew, paving the way for the subsequent rise of anti-gender mobilization.
</details>  

Gimbutaitė, Monika. "Cinemas in Transition: How Implicit Policy Affected the Network of Cinemas in Lithuania (1990–2001)." *Eastern European Screen Studies*, 5 Oct. 2026 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/29974828.2026.2739739).  
<details markdown="1"><summary>Abstract</summary>
This article explores how Lithuania’s network of cinemas transformed between 1990 and 2001 as the country’s film sector shifted from a centralized Soviet system to a market economy with fragmented governance. Following the transfer of cinemas to municipal control, the Ministry of Culture largely neglected the sector, leaving cinema operators to navigate financial, legal and technical challenges independently. The absence of a film policy strategy contributed to widespread privatization, closures, and the rise of informal circulatory networks. It also resulted in restricted access to financial support and unequal labour conditions. Drawing on archival sources, an analysis of media discourse, and Bourdieu’s theory, the article demonstrates how implicit policy allowed economic capital to prevail over cultural capital in relation to film exhibition infrastructure in Lithuania.
</details>  

Tavolja, Kädi. "Expressive, Intellectual, and In-between: Lithuanians, Estonians, and Latvians at the Vilnius Painting Triennials During the Soviet Era." *Acta Academiae Artium Vilnensis*, no. 122, 30 Sept. 2026 [<span style="background-color: #5a96d0; color: white; padding: 0.25em 0.4em; border-radius: 0.25rem; font-size: 75%; line-height: 1;">Open Access</span>](https://doi.org/10.37522/aaav.122.2026.367 ).  
<details markdown="1"><summary>Abstract</summary>
The article examines how the Vilnius Painting Triennials (1969–1987) shaped perceptions of Estonian, Latvian, and Lithuanian painting during the late Soviet period. Rather than treating the triennials primarily as manifestations of Baltic solidarity or cultural resistance to Soviet rule, the study argues that they functioned as a key comparative framework through which distinct national schools of art were constructed and negotiated. Drawing on close readings of exhibition reviews published in Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania, the article traces the formation and persistence of recurring interpretative stereotypes: Estonian painting as intellectual, restrained, and rational; Lithuanian painting as expressive, painterly, and emotionally charged; and Latvian painting as occupying an intermediate position between the two. It also explores how these characterizations reflected differing artistic value systems and critical traditions rather than objective stylistic realities.  
  
The analysis demonstrates that although the triennials fostered sustained artistic exchange, they also encouraged competition among the Baltic republics. Critics consistently interpreted their own national art through locally established aesthetic values while evaluating neighbouring schools from different perspectives. Over nearly two decades, these interpretative patterns proved remarkably resilient, even as artistic practices evolved. The article suggests that the persistence of such national typologies resulted not only from the structure of the triennials, organized as separate national presentations, but also from the formalist methodologies of art criticism itself. Ultimately, the study argues that Baltic art criticism contributed as much to the construction of national artistic identities as the artworks it sought to describe, while simultaneously reinforcing distinctions that often overshadowed shared artistic developments.
</details>  

Vanagaitė, Gitana. "The Significance of Imagination in Becoming a Nation: Poetry in Late Nineteenth- Century Lithuanian Newspapers Aušra and Varpas." *Interlitteraria*, vol. 31, no. 1, 17 Sept. 2026 [<span style="background-color: #5a96d0; color: white; padding: 0.25em 0.4em; border-radius: 0.25rem; font-size: 75%; line-height: 1;">Open Access</span>]( https://doi.org/10.12697/IL.2026.31.1.14 ).  
<details markdown="1"><summary>Abstract</summary>
This article examines the role of poetry published in the late nineteenth-century Lithuanian monthly periodicals Aušra (The Dawn, 1883– 1886) and Varpas (The Bell, 1889–1905) in shaping modern Lithuanian national consciousness. Although Aušra has traditionally been interpreted within the paradigm of Romanticism, the article argues that its poetry cannot be considered Romantic in the full aesthetic sense. Instead, it functioned primarily as an instrumental, socially oriented discourse that employed selected Romantic motifs in the service of nation-building.  
  
Drawing on theories of nationalism (Miroslav Hroch), narrative and cultural imagination (Martin Puchner), and productive imagination (Paul Ricoeur), the article analyses how poetic language in Aušra constructed an imagined homeland grounded in moral values, historical idealisation, and collective identity rather than individual subjectivity. Particular attention is given to prophetic and religious rhetoric, images of divine protection, and the figure of the committed individual, all of which together shaped practical models of ethical and social behaviour.  
  
While Aušra’s poetry relied on prophetic and moral imagination oriented toward collective awakening, Varpas transformed imagination into a productive civic force, shaping models of responsible action and individual commitment. Drawing on Paul Ricoeur’s concept of productive imagination, the article argues that poetry in Varpas no longer projects an ideal future but actively shapes models of civic action and responsibility.
</details>  

Ventsel, Aimar. " Sex Pistols in the Soviet Union: the Estonian Experience ." *The Routledge Handbook of the Sex Pistols*, 2026 (soon). [link](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/edit/10.4324/9781003489481-4/sex-pistols-soviet-union-aimar-ventsel)  
<details markdown="1"><summary>Annotation</summary>
In the Soviet Union, the Sex Pistols were the most important Western band to initiate and inspire the punk movement and had this position until the collapse of the Soviet Union in the early 1990s. In a sense, the Sex Pistols were central to the punk of the Soviet Union. Whereas for some in the West, the Sex Pistols either sold out or became irrelevant, in the Soviet Union, the band was a template for music, fashion, vocabulary and attitude. One reason for this was irregular access to Western music and other cultural information, while the language barrier contributed heavily to the construction of the understanding of ‘real punk’. The notion of the mainstream and underground differed from the West – in the Soviet Union, underground music was everything that was not played on the radio (in general, the notion of ‘commercial’ and ‘sold out’ had very different meanings in the Soviet Union). In addition, there was limited contact with punks from other countries, especially the West. Therefore, the understanding of the ‘authentic punk’ was based on music released in Western countries by major labels, information spread by mainstream music magazines and broadcasting (BBC, Finnish TV and radio, for some regions, Polish for others) and clothes sold in Western commercial outlets (or copied in a DIY way). Based on interviews and memories in Estonia, Russia and Ukraine, this chapter will discuss how and why the Sex Pistols gained the status of the ‘one and only’.
</details>  

 Olchikenova, Gulmira, Zhangylsyn  Kiyubek. "National Identity Politics and State Consolidation in Latvia and Estonia Since Independence." *Bulletin of the L.N. Gumilyov Eurasian National University. Political Science. Regional Studies. Oriental Studies. Turkology Series*, vol. 156, no. 3, 30.09.2026 [link](https://bulpolit.enu.kz/index.php/main/article/view/1081).  
<details markdown="1"><summary>Abstract</summary>
This article explores how Latvia and Estonia have addressed significant challenges related to forming national identity, consolidating their states, and distancing themselves from the Soviet legacy since regaining independence. The objective of the study is to analyze the key factors that have influenced nation-building in both countries, with a focus on language policy, memory politics, and the integration of Russian-speaking minorities. The research is qualitative in nature and draws upon academic literature, legal documents, statistical data, and policy reports. It incorporates elements of discourse analysis, historical comparison, and case-based examinations of political and cultural transformations. The findings indicate that the restoration of Latvian and Estonian statehood necessitated not only political and economic restructuring but also a reevaluation of cultural and linguistic foundations that were weakened during the Soviet era. The analysis reveals that citizenship laws, minority integration frameworks, and language reforms were pivotal in redefining national identity. Additionally, memory politics played a crucial role; interpretations of World War II, the removal of Soviet monuments, and disputes over historical narratives significantly influenced public discourse and state policies. The study highlights that, although Russian-speaking minorities initially faced exclusion, gradual policy adjustments and European integration have helped alleviate tensions. Nevertheless, Russia's geopolitical actions – including the annexation of Crimea and the full-scale invasion of Ukraine – have notably hastened the Baltic states' efforts to diminish Russian influence. This study emphasizes how small states with complex ethnolinguistic landscapes can effectively consolidate their nationhood. The experiences of Latvia and Estonia offer a valuable comparative model for understanding post-Soviet state-building, the politics of memory, and the challenges of managing historical legacies while integrating into European structures.
</details>

Kyuchukov), Huseyin S. Kyuchuk (Hristo, Urszula Wróblewska, Małgorzata Wenclik, Danuta Narbut. "“i Am Polish, I Speak Russian, but I Identify as Lithuanian”: Language Use and Identity Among Polish Minority Female University Students in Lithuania." *Edukacja Międzykulturowa*, vol. 2026, no. Nr 3(34), 2026 [DOI](https://doi.org/10.15804/em.2026.03.11).  
<details markdown="1"><summary>Abstract</summary>
The study investigates the relationship between language use and ethnic-cultural identity among female university students belonging to the Polish minority in Vilnius, Lithuania. Taking into account the theoretical models of Algis Norvilas and Ieva Valskytė-Janušaitienė (2007) and Kinga Geben and Meilutė Ramoniene (2015), the paper presents qualitative analyses of the data collected among female university students in Vilnius, Lithuania. The Polish community in Lithuania has a long historical presence and maintains a well-developed institutional infrastructure, including cultural organizations and educational institutions that provide instruction in the Polish language. The data for the present study were collected from an assignment written by pedagogy students enrolled in a course on Intercultural Communication at the Branch of the University of Bialystok in Vilnius. At the end of the semester, students were asked to compose a reflective assignment entitled “My Ethnic and Cultural Identity” in Polish, Russian, or English. The assignments were a result of an action research methodology (Norton, 2019; McNiff, 2014). The analysis reveals a complex relationship between linguistic practices and identity construction among the participants. The students reported using multiple languages in everyday communication and articulated diverse identity configurations. Five principal patterns emerged from the data: (1) Polish-Russian-speaking students identifying as Polish, (2) PolishLithuanian speakers identifying as Lithuanian, (3) Russian-speaking students identifying as Polish, (4) Polish-speaking students identifying as Lithuanian, and (5) Mixed multicultural nationalities identifying as Polish. Although the sample size is limited, the findings contribute innovative insights to the study of multilingual minority communities and identity. In particular, the results challenge the claim that language constitutes the primary determinant of ethnic identity, as proposed by Polly Louise Price (2010).
</details>  

Jiang, Haoxuan. *Nation-building Through Language: a Discourse Analysis of Estonian Education Policy*. 2026. University of Gothenburg. [<span style="background-color: #5a96d0; color: white; padding: 0.25em 0.4em; border-radius: 0.25rem; font-size: 75%; line-height: 1;">Open Access</span>](https://gupea.ub.gu.se/server/api/core/bitstreams/cfb34b98-cafc-4e95-a55f-96bcf52e53b9/content)  
<details markdown="1"><summary>Annotation</summary>
This thesis examines the role of language policy as an instrument of nation-building in Estonia. It focuses on the discursive construction of language, identity, and integration in official education policy documents. Within the broader context of post-Soviet nation-building, the study analyses how linguistic governance reflects historical legacies and current political priorities. The research is a qualitative discourse analysis, which employs Ruíz’s orientations of language planning and the Discourse-Historical Approach (DHA). A selection of national policy documents, including laws, the national curriculum, and education strategies, is analysed to identify recurring patterns. The findings indicate that Estonian is consistently positioned as a symbolic marker of national identity and as a prerequisite for civic participation. Minority language speakers are primarily framed as a challenge in the educational system. The findings demonstrate that language policy promotes inclusion while reinforcing linguistic hierarchies. The study contends that Estonian language policy exemplifies a broader post-Soviet trend. In this trend, language functions as a central mechanism for consolidating national identity and managing diversity. By foregrounding the discursive construction of policy, this thesis offers a deeper understanding of how nation-building processes are enacted through language education in contemporary governance.
</details>
